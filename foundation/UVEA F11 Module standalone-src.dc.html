UVEA · Foundations
F11 · Drug-induced

Why this matters

Three of the four buckets are treated by adding something. This one is treated by taking something away.

Identifying it can spare a patient years of immunosuppression they never needed.

It is also the bucket most often missed, for a mundane reason: the answer lives in a medication list, in a different chart, that nobody opened.

The case you will see

A 58-year-old is referred with three weeks of bilateral blurred vision and photophobia. There are anterior chamber cells in both eyes and some vitreous cell.

The referral says
Idiopathic bilateral uveitis, start steroids.

He is nine weeks into pembrolizumab for metastatic melanoma. Nobody in the eye pathway has been told that, because it is oncology's medication and it was not on the eye referral.

The diagnosis was available in the first minute of the encounter, and only to someone who asked.

The established systemic agents

Most strongly associated
The antiviral cidofovir and the antibiotic rifabutin, used against Mycobacterium avium complex, are the two systemic drugs most strongly linked to ocular inflammation including anterior uveitis. Bisphosphonates — pamidronate and alendronate among them — are highly associated with both scleritis and uveitis.
Moderately associated
Systemic fluoroquinolones, sulfonamides, and diethylcarbamazine. Moxifloxacin deserves separate mention because it can produce iris depigmentation as well as uveitis, and the depigmentation is striking enough to be mistaken for something else entirely.
The pattern worth noticing: these are drugs given for infection, for bone disease, and for parasites. None of them is a drug a patient thinks of as relevant to their eyes, and several are prescribed by teams the ophthalmologist never speaks to.

Checkpoint inhibitors

Cancer immunotherapy with immune checkpoint inhibitors has been associated with a spectrum of intraocular inflammation, and it is the fastest-growing entry in this bucket.

The spectrum is wide.
mild AU
panuveitis
It runs from mild anterior uveitis at one end to retinal vasculitis, choroiditis, and a Vogt-Koyanagi-Harada-like panuveitis at the other. That range is what makes it difficult: there is no single presentation to recognise, so the drug has to be found in the history rather than inferred from the eye.

The mechanism makes it predictable rather than surprising. These agents work by removing brakes on the immune response. Intraocular inflammation is that mechanism doing what it was designed to do, in a place nobody wanted it. A resident who understands that will think of the drug before they see the pattern.

Practical consequence: any new uveitis in a patient on cancer immunotherapy is this until shown otherwise.

The paradoxes and the local agents

Drugs that treat uveitis and have been associated with causing it
Some TNF inhibitors, etanercept and adalimumab among them, have been linked with new-onset uveitis, a psoriasis-like rash, and a systemic sarcoid-like syndrome. This is genuinely disorienting the first time you meet it.
Vaccines and tuberculin
BCG and influenza vaccines, and the purified protein derivative used in tuberculin skin testing, have all been implicated. Intravesical BCG, used in bladder cancer, can produce uveitis that may be immune-mediated or infectious — a distinction that matters.
Topical and intravitreal agents
Several ocular hypotensive drops are associated with uveitis: brimonidine, pilocarpine, and prostaglandin analogues. Brimonidine's presentation is characteristic — a red irritated eye with extensive, often granulomatous keratic precipitates and low-grade anterior uveitis in a patient on long-term treatment, sometimes with corneal oedema and vitreous inflammation.
Intravitreal antibiotics, urokinase, cidofovir, and the VEGF inhibitors brolucizumab and aflibercept have also been implicated.

Method

Interrogating a medication list

The skill in this module is a history technique, not a pattern recognition. Four things make the difference.

1
Ask for everything, not for medications
Patients routinely omit eye drops, injections given at another clinic, vaccines, infusions, and anything they consider a treatment rather than a drug. Someone on monthly infusions for cancer will often answer no when asked whether they take any medication.
2
Ask when each was started, and when the eye symptoms began
Without both dates you have a list rather than a differential.
3
Ask about recent changes as well as current agents
A drug stopped six weeks ago can still be the cause of an inflammation that started eight weeks ago.
4
Read the actual record
For oncology, rheumatology, and infectious diseases patients, the reliable list is in their chart and not in their memory. This is the single highest-yield five minutes in the encounter, and it is the step that gets skipped when clinic is busy.

Narrowing it down

This bucket is a diagnosis of exclusion, and the exclusion is not optional. A drug association does not stop a patient having something else, and several of the drugs on this list are given to people at high risk of infectious uveitis.

Infectious first, as always
Rifabutin is given for mycobacterial infection. Cidofovir is given for CMV. Checkpoint inhibitors are given to people who may be immunocompromised for other reasons. In each case the indication for the drug is itself a risk factor for the differential you are trying to exclude — which makes this the bucket where anchoring is most dangerous.
Masquerade stays open too
A patient on cancer immunotherapy has cancer. Intraocular metastasis and paraneoplastic processes belong in the differential alongside the drug reaction.
A treponemal-specific syphilis test still applies here, as it does everywhere else.
Method

Reasoning about causality

There is no confirmatory test. What you have is a temporal association, and the module should be honest that this is weaker evidence than a diagnosis usually rests on.

What strengthens the case
A plausible interval between starting the drug and the inflammation; a presentation that matches what has been reported for that agent; the absence of a better explanation after the other three buckets have been worked; and improvement after the drug stops.
What does not
The absence of an alternative diagnosis on its own. Idiopathic uveitis is common. A patient can take rifabutin and have idiopathic disease at the same time, and the drug will look guilty because it is the only named thing in the room.
Rechallenge is the strongest evidence available and is usually neither ethical nor practical — which is precisely why the reasoning has to be documented rather than assumed.

Management principles

Discontinuation may be curative, which is why identifying the agent matters. But the decision to stop is not yours alone, and for several of these drugs it is not primarily an ophthalmic calculation at all.

A glaucoma drop or a fluoroquinolone
Substitution is usually straightforward and the conversation is short.
Cancer immunotherapy
It is not. The drug may be extending the patient's life. The question that goes to oncology is not whether to stop it but whether the ocular inflammation can be controlled while it continues — and that framing matters, because an ophthalmologist who asks for discontinuation without it will sometimes be right and will often be ignored.
TNF inhibitors
The paradox has to be stated plainly to the referring rheumatologist, since the natural assumption will be that the uveitis represents inadequate treatment rather than the treatment itself.

Pearls and pitfalls

✓ Any new uveitis in a patient on cancer immunotherapy is drug-related until shown otherwise. The spectrum is too wide to recognise by pattern.
✓ Patients do not count drops, injections, infusions, or vaccines as medication. Ask for all of them by name.
✓ Brimonidine has a recognisable presentation. Granulomatous keratic precipitates and low-grade anterior uveitis in a long-term glaucoma patient should prompt a look at the drop bottle before a systemic workup.
✓ The indication is a risk factor for the differential. Rifabutin means mycobacterial disease; cidofovir means CMV. The drug and its reason both belong in the differential.
! Pitfall. Treating absence of another diagnosis as evidence of drug causation. Idiopathic uveitis is common, and a named drug will always look more guilty than an unnamed cause.
! Pitfall. Asking oncology to stop the drug rather than asking whether the eye can be controlled alongside it. The first request is often declined; the second usually starts a useful conversation.

Check yourself

Answer each before revealing. The reasoning matters more than the answer.

1. A patient on long-term brimonidine has a red irritated eye with extensive granulomatous keratic precipitates and low-grade anterior uveitis. What is the first move?
Look at the drops. This is the characteristic brimonidine presentation, and granulomatous keratic precipitates in a long-term glaucoma patient should point at the bottle before they point at a sarcoidosis workup. Corneal oedema and vitreous inflammation may accompany it.
2. Why is anchoring particularly dangerous in this bucket?
Because the indication for the drug is often a risk factor for what you are trying to exclude. Rifabutin is given for mycobacterial infection, cidofovir for CMV, checkpoint inhibitors to patients who may be immunocompromised. Finding the drug does not release you from clearing the infectious bucket — it makes doing so more important.
3. A patient's uveitis started two weeks after beginning a drug, and no other cause is found. How strong is the case?
Moderate at best. A plausible interval and a matching reported presentation support it; the absence of an alternative diagnosis does not, on its own, because idiopathic uveitis is common. Improvement on withdrawal strengthens it. Rechallenge would settle it and is usually neither practical nor ethical.
4. A rheumatologist refers a patient on adalimumab with new uveitis, assuming the arthritis treatment is failing. What might be happening instead?
The drug itself may be the cause. Some TNF inhibitors, including adalimumab and etanercept, have been associated with new-onset uveitis, a psoriasis-like rash, and a sarcoid-like systemic syndrome — a paradox worth stating plainly, because the natural assumption runs the other way.
5. What question should go to oncology about a patient with checkpoint-inhibitor-associated uveitis?
Not whether to stop the drug, but whether the eye disease can be controlled while it continues. The agent may be extending the patient's life, so the calculus is not primarily ophthalmic — and a request framed as discontinuation will often simply be declined.
