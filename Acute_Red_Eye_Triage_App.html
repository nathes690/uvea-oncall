The medication review

The inflammation looks like primary, isolated anterior uveitis. Before ordering anything, ask what the patient is taking — this is the most commonly overlooked category, and often the curable one.

Next question

Is this a bilateral, granulomatous, or hypopyon uveitis in a patient on chronic therapy — topical glaucoma drops, a checkpoint inhibitor, a BRAF/MEK inhibitor, a bisphosphonate, rifabutin, or another reported agent?

A · NOT ISOLATED
Recognize

With a corneal ulcer, infiltrate, or dendrite; a recent procedure, injection, or bleb; penetrating trauma; or posterior involvement, this is most likely not a primary, isolated anterior uveitis.

Escalate

Consider contacting Cornea, Retina, or Uveitis — by the finding (± Infectious Diseases).

This is where the wrong label does the most harm — keratitis, endophthalmitis, traumatic inflammation, or a posterior/panuveitis each go to a different service and several are time-critical. Hypopyon with prominent AC cell should raise endophthalmitis. Sorting this out first protects the rest of the tree.

Corneal findings, sensation, contact-lens history
Procedure or trauma type & date; wound or bleb integrity
Hypopyon, vision, pain, IOP in both eyes
Posterior findings — B-scan when there is no view
Test Consider a treponemal-specific test with the attending — syphilis presents at either end of this branch; add cultures or directed PCR where endophthalmitis is on the table.

Supports recognition and escalation only. Culture, tap-and-inject decisions, and antimicrobial choice follow institutional protocols and attending consultation. No doses.

B · MEDICATION
Recognize

A bilateral, granulomatous, or hypopyon anterior uveitis in a patient on chronic therapy should prompt a medication review — the most overlooked, and often reversible, category.

Escalate

Consider contacting Uveitis (± the prescribing service — for oncologic agents, continuing the drug is oncology's call).

The category with the best prognosis — excellent, once the agent is withdrawn — and the cheapest to check. The classic trap is a sarcoid workup on a glaucoma patient with granulomatous uveitis without first looking at the drop bottle. Causation is confirmed by dechallenge with resolution.

Full medication list with start dates — the interval matters
Glaucoma drops (brimonidine, prostaglandins); checkpoint inhibitors; BRAF/MEK; bisphosphonates; rifabutin
Laterality, KP size & type, hypopyon, IOP both eyes
Any concurrent immune-related event — vitiligo, colitis, hearing loss
Test A treponemal-specific test still applies — syphilis presents as anterior uveitis regardless of a plausible drug cause.

Supports recognition and escalation only. These agents are named as etiologies to recognize, not instructions. Whether any medication is held or continued is a decision for the attending and prescribing service.

C · VIRAL
Recognize

A unilateral, hypertensive anterior uveitis with KPs, sectoral iris atrophy or transillumination defects, or reduced corneal sensation — especially with prior HZO or dendritic keratitis — is most consistent with viral AU (HSV, VZV, CMV).

Escalate

Consider contacting Uveitis (± Cornea if keratitis or endotheliitis).

Hand-off If the IOP is elevated, hand off to the Hypertensive Uveitis Triage module for the pressure decision.

The overlap with non-infectious hypertensive uveitis is real, and the consequence runs one way: corticosteroids without antiviral cover in an eye with an undiagnosed infection. The threat in CMV and Fuchs is progressive endothelial loss and a frequently surgical glaucoma. Where the picture is suspicious but not pathognomonic, an AC tap settles it.

Laterality & recurrence; KPs; iris atrophy / TIDs; corneal sensation; prior HZO; IOP both eyes; gonioscopy
Consider AC paracentesis for directed PCR (HSV/VZV/CMV) with the attending — a negative PCR does not exclude disease
A treponemal-specific test still applies — syphilis mimics viral disease readily

Supports recognition and escalation only. The decision to tap, and any antiviral or anti-inflammatory therapy, follow institutional protocols and attending consultation. No doses.

D · DEFAULT
Recognize

No corneal, surgical, traumatic, or posterior cause; no culprit medication; no viral features — consistent with a non-infectious or idiopathic, undifferentiated anterior uveitis.

Escalate

Consider contacting Uveitis.

HLA-B27, sarcoidosis, Behçet, TINU, JIA — or idiopathic. The instinct to send a shotgun panel is the expensive mistake: history, medication review, and slit-lamp do most of the work; testing should confirm a hypothesis. Idiopathic is the most common outcome of a complete negative workup — an acceptable place to land, not a failure.

Onset & duration; laterality & alternating episodes (→ HLA-B27); granulomatous vs non-granulomatous KPs
Hypopyon character — fibrinous, non-mobile → HLA-B27; mobile, "cold" → Behçet
Targeted ROS — back pain, psoriasis, IBD, oral/genital ulcers, TB exposure
Test A treponemal-specific test in essentially everyone; then HLA-B27, chest imaging, ANA/RF, TINU labs, or TB testing — each only where history and exam raised the hypothesis.

Supports recognition, testing orientation, and escalation only. Corticosteroid, cycloplegic, and any systemic immunomodulatory decisions follow institutional protocols and attending consultation.
