STEP 1 OF 3

Check an APD first

Every patient. Before attributing loss to the inflammation you can see, check a relative APD — an optic neuropathy can hide behind uveitis.

First question

Is there a relative APD, or is the vision loss out of proportion to the exam with optic-nerve features (dyschromatopsia, disc edema or pallor)?

STEP 2 OF 3

A same-night emergency?

No optic neuropathy on the pupil exam. The next question is whether the retina or CNS shows something that cannot wait until morning.

Next question

Is there dense retinal whitening or peripheral necrotizing retinitis; a retinal artery or vein occlusion; or CNS symptoms (headache, focal deficit) with placoid lesions?

STEP 3 OF 3

Explained by what you see?

No tonight-emergency. Separate the two remaining pictures: is the loss accounted for by the inflammation in front of you, or is there something subtler in the posterior pole?

Next question

Is the vision loss fully explained by dense anterior chamber or vitreous inflammation you can already see (hypopyon, dense cell, media opacity)?

A · TIME-CRITICAL
Recognize

An APD, or vision loss out of proportion to the exam with optic-nerve features, points to an optic neuropathy accompanying the inflammation — not a purely retinal cause.

Escalate & flag imaging

Consider, with the attending, MRI of the brain and orbits with thin cuts through the optic nerve (± contrast). Contact uveitis + neuro-ophthalmology.

An optic neuropathy can hide behind a diagnosis of "uveitis," and the APD is the single most reliable bedside signal that the nerve is involved. Inflammatory and compressive neuropathies both occur here — orbital disease in GPA, for example — and loss out of proportion to the fundus is the clue that dedicated imaging of the nerve is needed.

APD & grade, color vision, confrontation fields, disc appearance
Degree of acuity-versus-fundus mismatch
Systemic vasculitis review (sinonasal, pulmonary, renal, joint, skin)

Supports recognition and escalation only. Imaging choice and timing, and any treatment, follow institutional protocols and attending consultation.

B · TIME-CRITICAL
Recognize

Necrotizing retinitis, a retinal artery or vein occlusion, or CNS symptoms with placoid lesions make acute inflammatory vision loss a same-night emergency.

Escalate now

Consider, with the attending, urgent uveitis/retina evaluation tonight; when CNS symptoms accompany placoid lesions, urgent neurology (incl. neuro-imaging and CSF). ± Infectious diseases.

These are the sight- and life-threatening causes. A necrotizing retinitis can progress to detachment and to the fellow eye within days; a vascular occlusion threatens vision now and can mark systemic vasculitis; and placoid lesions with headache or a focal deficit (APMPPE–cerebral vasculitis) are a neurologic emergency.

Extent of whitening/necrosis; arterial vs venous occlusion; vitritis
CNS symptoms (headache, focal deficit) and their tempo
State of the fellow eye

Supports recognition and escalation only. Antimicrobial, antiviral, corticosteroid and other treatment decisions follow institutional protocols and attending consultation.

C · FLAG FOR IMAGING
Recognize · centerpiece

Loss not explained by the nerve, an emergency, or the anterior inflammation you can see — consider a posterior uveitis. A working label is enough.

Escalate & flag imaging

Escalate to uveitis/retina with the explicit message that the patient may benefit from OCT, fluorescein angiography, and/or ICG. Consider, with the attending, a treponemal-specific test.

The tools that make these diagnoses — OCT, fluorescein angiography, ICG — are usually not available in the ER, and on FA/ICG the abnormalities are often more numerous than the visible lesions. The job is not to name the entity but to notice the subtle findings and make sure uveitis/retina knows the patient may benefit from ancillary imaging.

Faint placoid/deep white lesions & distribution; any evolving rapidly; mild vitritis; disc edema
Symptoms outstripping the exam — photopsias, scotomata, blind-spot enlargement
Laterality and any fellow-eye change

Supports recognition and escalation for imaging. Naming the entity, and any treatment, follow the imaging, institutional protocols, and retina/uveitis consultation.

D · DEFAULT / HAND-OFF
Recognize

The loss is fully accounted for by dense anterior chamber or vitreous inflammation already visible — a hypopyon, heavy cell and flare, or a media opacity.

Hand off

A hypopyon or dense AC reaction is carried in depth by the Hypopyon Triage and Acute Red Eye with Anterior Chamber Inflammation modules. Contact uveitis via those pathways.

Here the vision loss is a downstream consequence of inflammation you can already see. The one trap: don't treat "explained by the vitritis" as the end of the thought — dense vitritis or media opacity can hide a necrotizing retinitis, so this reading is only safe once the posterior pole is cleared.

AC cell, flare, hypopyon; vitritis density & quality of the posterior view; IOP
A B-scan when there is no posterior view — don't let it mask a retinitis
Scope: acute loss only — gradual decline (cataract, ERM) is a clinic question

Supports recognition and escalation only. The anterior-segment management itself is handled by the modules named above and follows institutional protocols and attending consultation.
